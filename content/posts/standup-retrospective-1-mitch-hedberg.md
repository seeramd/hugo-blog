I'm a comedian, which means I am a fan of comedy. I've watched/listened to hundreds of comedy performances that have shaped my ideals of stand-up comedy and my own performance style. I figured a good exercise would be to revisit some old records, share some of my thoughts, and maybe learn some history along the way. I can wax philosophical about comedy all day, which is good because everyone knows comedy gets funnier the more you explain it.

Mitch Hedberg is my favorite comedian, which I stand by regardless of it being cliche. You would be hard pressed to find a comic who does not cite Hedberg as a direct influence. In some ways he is the Kurt Cobain of comedy: a blonde artist with a drug problem who launched a cult following and thousands of imitators. Among comedy fans, he occupies the highest of pedestals, and has been deified to perhaps an excessive degree. In a society where we regularly exchange ideas through memetic snippets of text and video, Hedberg's one liners are primed for out of context regurgitation by die hard fans in web forums and comment sections. As a virtually universally beloved performer, he's also fertile ground for farming "praising the thing that you like" content on social media. I realize it's starting to sound like I hate the guy. Listen, I used to love Mitch Hedberg's comedy. I still do, but I used to too.

Let's cut through the second hand discussion and go straight to the source material. *Strategic Grill Locations*, Hedberg's first album, was recorded on September 7th, 1999 at the Laff Stop in Houston, Texas. It takes its name from another one of his jokes that does not appear on the album. The Laff Stop was a comedy club franchise with locations in California and Texas. The Houston location where this CD was recorded (and many others by Doug Stanhope, Bill Hicks, Dane Cook etc.) closed in late 2009. The Austin, Texas Laff Stop is now the Cap City Comedy Club. I can't find any records on what happened to the CA locations, but they seem to be gone with the wind as well.

The CD was initially self-published and sold on his website. I'm fairly certain his original website was www.mitchhedberg.com; this is the website on the liner notes of the original CD. Later, after Hedberg's passing, his estate would launch www.mitchhedberg.net, which is said to have featured clips, stories, and other media. At time of writing, both of these sites are defunct and the domains are for sale. Internet Archive is currently unable to retrieve anything from either site. Hedberg explains his motivation for self publishing in a phone interview with Ryan of the [Revolt of the Apes Blog](https://revoltoftheapes.com/2010/05/11/funny-flashback-mitch-hedberg/):

>Yeah. Well, I was looking for a label, but couldn’t find one, so I just put it out myself. There was enough of a demand for one, and plus when you sell it one your own, you keep the profits, so I don’t mind having it on a real label. I like just doing things myself. My manager was worried it would end up in a bargain bin somewhere.


It took a few years, but it was in fact not destined for the bargain bin. In 2002, Comedy Central Records re-issued the CD to coincide with the release of his second album, *Mitch All Together.* The re-release featured different cover art, and was also an edited down version of the original recording, seemingly contrary to Hedberg's original intentions:

>Well, I thought I would edit it down but then decided, fuck it. Because, A) in order to edit it, you actually have to do something, and B) it keeps the experience of the actual show. When people see me on Letterman or HBO, it’s just five minutes of jokes I know are going to work, so I thought it would be nice to have that didn’t work, to have some smaller jokes, too.


These days, a comic self releasing and producing a comedy special or album is quite common, facilitated through social media and platforms like YouTube and Spotify. The comic is more equipped than ever to build their own following and use their own resources, circumventing traditional kingmakers such as HBO or Netflix. A less charitable interpretation of this current arrangement might be that the individual comic is *compelled* to expend their own social and monetary capital, while the gatekeepers of old are enamored with legacy acts and TikTok stars. My doomer-ism aside, self-releasing in the 90s would have been a much different animal. Decent fidelity video recording would have been quite expensive compared to today (and it's still pretty expensive today!), so the independent artist would have opted for an audio only album. And they'd have to burn their own CDs! How quaint. As much as I like to imagine Mitch booting up Nero, the self-release CDs were manufactured by a company called Disc Makers according to the liner notes, which is surprisingly [still around](https://www.discmakers.com/).

Above is the original cover art made by Mitch's wife, Lynn Shawcroft, followed by the cover of the Comedy Central re-release on the right. The photo used in the new cover was also taken by Shawcroft. The original recording is a little hard to find if you don't have the CD; you won't find it on any streaming platforms. The original CD is still floating around online shopping sites. Just between you and me, there is also a rip uploaded to YouTube by a bootlegging channel [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ScqbJaqzlcA). There's about 10 minutes of extra audio, maybe I'll break down the differences in a future post. The rest of this post will focus on the Comedy Central rerelease, the version I'd wager most have heard.

Here's an excerpt text on the inside of the CD cover:


>Thanks to: Lynn Shawcroft, Dave Becky, Arne & Mary, Mark Babbit, The Laff Stop in Houston, Charlie Sanders, Michael O'Brien, Mark Landesman, and Ehren Katzur
>
>Recorded at the Laff Stop, Houston on September 7, 1999
>Stand-up bass by Chuck Savage


Some figures of note: Dave Becky was Mitch's manager ([and is still a prolific comedy manager and producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dave_Becky)). Arne and Mary were Mitch's parents. Mark Babbit was the manager of the Houston Laff Stop, and later the Houston Improv. His voice is the one you hear introducing Mitch at the beginning of the record. I'm going to highlight "Ehren Katzur" because I still have no idea who that is. Hey, did that say "Stand-up bass?" We should probably get to the actual content of the album. 

The first thing you will hear, after Babbit's intro and the crowd's applause, is the aforementioned stand-up bass. No, that is not an obscure stand-up comedy term, it is literally a bass line played by Charlie Sanders a.k.a [Chuck Savage](https://www.discogs.com/artist/1496109-Charlie-Sanders-Jr). Early on, Mitch addresses Chuck directly:

>...So don't fuck up the bass tonight, Chuck, cause we're on a CD here.

The bass is played throughout, and is one of the most distinctive qualities of the album. It indubitably meshes perfectly with Mitch's laid back style and delivery, giving the performance a beat poetry vibe. Although, Mitch seems to take umbrage with this categorization:

>Sometimes I have a bass player on stage with me and do jokes over a bass rhythm, and people think I’m like a jazz comic, but I don’t want people to think I’m trying to do some beatnik bullshit.

So what motivated this creative choice? I've wondered for years, but it seems I don't need to wonder any longer. About 4 months ago from time of writing, a user claiming to be Chuck Savage himself [posted on the Mitch Hedberg subreddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/MitchHedberg/comments/1t4rw7r/hey/)! Assuming the post to be legitimate (he posted some images that don't come up in a reverse search, so I am inclined to think so) Chuck is a comic himself as well as a bass player. In a comment, he explains the origin of the bass:

>The club owner in Houston place called the Laff stop… His name was Mark Babbitt… It was his idea. He had been to a wedding and there was a band up there and Mitch was telling a few jokes and Mitch was messing with the Bass Player and Babbitt knew me as a comic that used it upright base and stuck us together.


Mystery solved, if this Reddit post is to be believed. Chuck would not make an appearance on Mitch's second album, for whatever reason, though they would continue to tour "on and off" together for the next several years. Chuck is still out there making music, and you can check out his bandcamp [here](https://chucksavage.bandcamp.com/). A few months later, he did another AMA on /r/standup and /r/comedy under a different user account. Most of the Q&As covered the same information, but he did also mention he wrote a book about his experience with Mitch, which is available on [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H48XHMMN).

As the album proceeds, the self produced aspect of the album is very apparent. Mitch, the bass, and the crowd are all adequately audible, but there is an unmistakable "LoFi" quality compared to his later albums. Outside of that it is, quite literally, vintage Mitch Hedberg. What that is exactly is sometimes hard to describe. I think it may help to describe through contrast with another famous one liner comic, Steven Wright. From a bird's eye view, they are superficially similar. They both deliver absurd one-liners with a distinctive, low energy cadence. However, where Wright has a robotic and detached delivery, Hedberg's delivery is casual and conversational. Where Wright can veer into full blown surrealism and abstract situations, Hedberg is also a bit surreal but more grounded in unorthodox observations and conclusions from the mundane. To summarize, Steven Wright is like a being from a different dimension, and Mitch Hedberg is like your laid back friend who sees our world in a unique way.

Another defining aspect of Mitch's performance in this album and in general is his vulnerability on stage. Not so much in his material, but in how he reacts to how the performance is going. He is quite openly self-critical, and candidly riffs off of jokes that don't seem to land fully with the audience.


>Alright. That's not a full joke there. That's filler...


There is palpable nervousness in his stage presence; it is well known that Mitch struggled with onstage anxiety and stage fright. You can see this very clearly in the following video where the jokes aren't hitting, where he physically shrinks behind the mic stand and avoids eye contact with the audience.

{{< youtube "T3CTvdVz0jY" >}}

I can tell you first hand that bombing sucks, but Mitch was acutely affected by it. I speculate that it is part of the reason why Mark Babbit thought to include the stand-up bass in the performance; the music might've helped fill in any silence and ease Mitch's nerves. Coupled with his friendly persona, his vulnerability is an undeniably endearing quality. You can't help but root for the guy. I'd compare it to watching a knuckleballer in baseball; the style is so unique that you love seeing it do well (funny coincidence, one of the last knuckleball pitchers in baseball was named Steven Wright). I realize I might be making it sound like this album is a rough showing for Mitch. Make no mistake, he is killing it here. The Houston Laff Stop crowd was known for being hot, and they are hanging on every word. And Chuck, in fact, does not "fuck up the bass".

Earlier in this post, I was perhaps expressing some exhaustion with the endless parade of quoting and praise Mitch Hedberg receives online. After re-visiting this album, I think I've concluded that I feel this way because the parroting pales in comparison to the genuine article. His voice is easy to mimic and his jokes are easy to quote, but when he delivers them they are infused with his unique personality and inimitable creativity. It's one of the purest examples of that old adage about saying funny things versus saying things funny. In my opinion, it's not just about having a funny voice or act. Even if you are playing a heightened character, your performance should capture who you are; that is the element of your act that the audience can't get from anyone else. I think it ties into why Mitch originally opted for an unedited cut of the recording, and why even in the cut down version we still get some of the more tepid reactions and riffs thereof. It's "the experience of the actual show," where we get the full portrait of Hedberg as a comic and a person.

Anyhow, I'm off to bomb at an open mic. Let me know your thoughts, or if you can tell me who in the world Ehren Katzur is.
