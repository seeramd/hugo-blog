I recently finished reading the *Adi Parva* (lit., the beginning book) of the Indian epic *The Mahabharata*. This covers 236 chapters. I started this endeavor in an effort to connect with my Hindu origins, however at this point I feel the need to nurse a yearning for Americana, absurd as it may sound. I subscribe not to nationalism or exceptionalism; show me a patriot and I'll show you a fool. However, it is the land of my birth, and it would be just as foolish to fully divorce myself from the legacy of the United States. In sin this country was conceived, and I inherit the glory and the iniquity. All this to explain my decision to read a popular candidate for the "great American novel": *Moby Dick*.

## Chapter 1 - Loomings

Based on the mythology of Moby Dick that permeates popular culture, I had constructed the idea of Ishmael as a passive narrator, an oculus through which the reader observes the struggle between Ahab and the titular whale. In stark contrast, Ishmael and his myriad opinions, expositions, and digressions take center stage. The famed sea captain and his quarry are nowhere to be found in this chapter, and it appears they will not be seen for some time. This chapter serves as a diatribe explaining Ishmael's desire to work on a whaling ship, and he expresses the impetus behind his wanderlust in a curious way:

*"Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul; whenever I find myself involuntarily pausing before coffin warehouses, and bringing up the rear of every funeral I meet... I account it high time to get to sea as soon as I can"*

Where Don Quixote was motivated by delusion, and Crusoe by calamity, our Ishmael is called to adventure by depression. It seems almost anachronistic to read such frank and irreverent discussion of suicidal ideation in a mid 19th century novel; such meditations would not be out of place in the Twitter post history of a 20-something Zoomer. Perhaps this feeling is informed by Gen Z's occasional memetic proclivity for antiquity (Ea-nāṣir, "the children yearn for the mines," and the like). However, I, as Ishmael is inclined to do, digress.

We start in Manhattan. On the subject of yearning, Ishmael pontificates at length on all peoples' supposed fundamental desire for the sea.

*"Go from Corlears Hook to Coenties Slip, and from thence, by Whitehall northward. What do you see?—Posted like silent sentinels all around the town, stand thousands upon thousands of mortal men fixed in ocean reveries
...
Why is almost every robust healthy boy with a robust healthy soul in him, at some time or other crazy to go to sea?"*

I have, in fact, worked in lower Manhattan. I must confess that I too have gazed longingly out a top floor highrise toward the Hudson River, gloriously emblazoned with the ochre hues of sunset. Or at least I did, before that view was abruptly snuffed out by the construction of a luxury condominium. The regions Ishmael highlights in the above passage form parts of the Financial District, or "FiDi" as it has been dubbed by real estate developers and quantitative traders. Do such "people" yearn for the sea? Can their soul feel the ocean breeze from behind a Bloomberg terminal?

Ishmael emphatically clarifies that he does not wish to go to sea as a passenger, but as a sailor. Not just any sailor, but a "simple" sailor without any trapping of an officer or otherwise esteemed (potentially cushy) position. No, Ishmael is a proud member of the sailing proletariat. He presents a romanticized depiction of this sort of blue collar vie Bohème, which, to my reading, betrays his youth and relative inexperience.

*"For as in this world, head winds are far more prevalent than winds from astern... so for the most part the Commodore on the quarter-deck gets his atmosphere at second hand from the sailors on the forecastle."*

One might imagine a harpooner of a dozen or so years might have a different view than this wet behind-the-ears sailor that has thus far only swabbed the deck of merchant vessels. It is hard not to be charmed by his enthusiasm, however, as Ishmael imagines his place in the "grande programme of Providence."

We close this chapter with a contemplation of the whale itself. As described, the whale is an entity of mystery and danger, a creature of biblical proportion that pulls men to the remotest corners of the Earth, where one may witness hitherto unseen horror and wonder in their pursuit. It is a quaint description in the modern context. The whaling industry peaked roughly when this book was published, and would steeply decline in the decades that followed. Central to this decline was the impact of overfishing; depleting whale populations rendered voyages like Ishmael's risky and unprofitable. Since 1982, commercial whaling has been almost entirely banned for conservation reasons, and to this day whale populations are still recovering.

Melville was not privy to this future, of course. It lands on us to reconcile this image of the leviathan, the "grand hooded phantom" against the reality of a hapless creature that is powerless to resist the frenzy of haphazard human consumption. With all the corners of the world being mapped, and my wallet as strained as Ishmael's, these pages are the only uncharted waters available to me. Onto Chapter 2.
